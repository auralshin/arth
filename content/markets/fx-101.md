### FX 101

> info **Metadata** Level: Beginner | Prerequisites: None | Tags: fx, currencies, quoting, spot, settlement, crosses

Foreign exchange is the only major market with no asset. Every position is a simultaneous long in one currency and a short in another, and the "price" is a ratio between two things that both move. There is no equivalent of holding cash and waiting: even doing nothing is a position, denominated in whatever currency your book is reported in.

It is also the largest market by turnover and one of the most convention-heavy. Almost every mistake a newcomer makes in FX is a convention error rather than a modelling error — the wrong side of a quote, the wrong direction of a cross, the wrong number of decimal places, or a settlement date that falls on a holiday in one of the two countries.

---

#### Quoting Conventions

A currency pair is written `BASE/QUOTE`, and the number is **how many units of the quote currency buy one unit of the base**.

```text
EUR/USD = 1.0850    means 1 euro costs 1.0850 US dollars
USD/JPY = 157.20    means 1 US dollar costs 157.20 yen
```

- Buying EUR/USD means buying euros and selling dollars.
- If EUR/USD rises, the euro has strengthened against the dollar.
- The **base** currency is the one you are long when you buy; the **quote** currency (also called the terms currency) is what the price is expressed in, and it is the currency your profit or loss naturally arrives in.

Which currency is the base is fixed by market convention, not by choice. The conventional precedence is EUR, then GBP, then AUD, then NZD, then USD, then everything else — so the market quotes EUR/USD and GBP/USD, but USD/JPY, USD/CHF and USD/CAD. Pairs where the dollar is the base are said to be quoted in **European terms**; pairs where it is the quote currency are in **American terms**. Reading a rate the wrong way round inverts the sign of every subsequent calculation.

A **pip** is the smallest conventional increment: the fourth decimal place for most pairs, the second decimal place for yen pairs. Many venues quote a fractional pip beyond that.

#### Worked Example: A Round Trip and Its Pips

The market shows EUR/USD at **1.0850 / 1.0852**. The lower number is the bid — the price at which the dealer buys the base currency from you. The higher number is the ask, at which the dealer sells it to you. You always trade on the side that is worse for you.

<table>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Step</strong></td><td><strong>Action</strong></td><td><strong>Rate used</strong></td><td><strong>USD flow</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><td>1</td><td>Buy EUR 1,000,000</td><td>Ask 1.0852</td><td>-1,085,200</td></tr>
    <tr><td>2</td><td>Later, sell EUR 1,000,000</td><td>Bid 1.0895</td><td>+1,089,500</td></tr>
    <tr><td>3</td><td>Net</td><td>—</td><td>+4,300</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Cross-checking in pips: the move from 1.0852 to 1.0895 is `0.0043`, which is 43 pips. For a EUR 1,000,000 position, one pip is worth `0.0001 * 1,000,000 = 100` US dollars, so `43 * 100 = 4,300`. The two calculations agree.

Note what has just happened to the book. The profit is 4,300 **US dollars**. If the fund reports in euros, that profit is itself an FX exposure until it is converted, at whatever rate prevails then. Unconverted P&L in a foreign currency is an open position, and books that ignore this discover it at the month-end revaluation.

#### Crosses

A **cross** is a pair not involving the US dollar. Historically these were computed through the dollar; today the majors trade directly, but the arithmetic still holds and still governs the spread.

```text
EUR/JPY = EUR/USD * USD/JPY
```

With EUR/USD at 1.0850 and USD/JPY at 157.20, the mid cross is `1.0850 * 157.20 = 170.5620`.

Now propagate the two-sided quotes. To buy EUR/JPY you buy EUR with USD (pay the EUR/USD ask, 1.0852) and buy USD with JPY (pay the USD/JPY ask, 157.24):

<table>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Quote</strong></td><td><strong>Bid</strong></td><td><strong>Ask</strong></td><td><strong>Spread</strong></td><td><strong>Spread in bp of mid</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><td>EUR/USD</td><td>1.0850</td><td>1.0852</td><td>0.0002 (2 pips)</td><td>1.84</td></tr>
    <tr><td>USD/JPY</td><td>157.20</td><td>157.24</td><td>0.04 (4 pips)</td><td>2.54</td></tr>
    <tr><td>EUR/JPY implied</td><td>170.5620</td><td>170.6368</td><td>0.0748 (7.5 pips)</td><td>4.38</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

The bid is `1.0850 * 157.20 = 170.5620` and the ask is `1.0852 * 157.24 = 170.6368`. In basis points of the mid, `1.84 + 2.54 = 4.38`, which is exactly the implied cross spread — **relative spreads add when you chain quotes**. This is why crosses are wider than majors, and why a strategy that trades three pairs to express one view pays three spreads.

> info **Triangular consistency is enforced by arbitrage, not by definition** If the directly quoted EUR/JPY drifts outside the range implied by EUR/USD and USD/JPY by more than transaction costs, the mismatch is arbitraged away in milliseconds. Consistency in observed data is therefore a sign the data is timestamped correctly; visible triangular profits in a backtest almost always indicate stale or unsynchronised quotes.

#### Spot, Forwards and Settlement

**Spot** does not mean immediate. The standard spot value date is `T+2` — two business days after the trade — with `T+1` for USD/CAD and a few others. The value date must be a business day in *both* currencies' home centres and, for pairs against the dollar, generally in New York too, so a US holiday can move the value date of a EUR/JPY trade.

**Tom-next** and other short-dated swaps exist to move a value date forward or back by a day. This is how a spot position is held beyond its value date without ever taking delivery: it is rolled each day, and the roll cost is the interest rate differential for one day. Retail platforms report this as an overnight financing charge; it is the same thing.

**Forward outright** is a rate for a value date beyond spot:

```text
forward_rate = spot_rate + forward_points
```

Forward points are quoted in pips and are determined entirely by the interest rate differential between the two currencies, not by any forecast. That mechanism is the subject of [FX Carry and Parity](/markets/fx-carry-parity).

**Settlement risk** is the genuinely distinctive FX hazard: because the two legs settle in different time zones, one party can pay before receiving. Payment-versus-payment settlement systems exist to eliminate this for the currencies they cover, but not all currencies and not all counterparties are covered.

#### Across Regions and Venues

**Majors.** Continuous trading from the Asian open on Monday to the New York close on Friday, with liquidity concentrated in the London-New York overlap. Spreads on the largest pairs are among the tightest in any market.

**Emerging market currencies.** Onshore markets may be closed to foreign participants entirely, in which case exposure trades as a **non-deliverable forward**: a contract cash-settled in dollars against an official fixing, with no local currency ever changing hands. The fixing itself becomes the tradable object, with all the incentive problems that implies.

**Pegged and managed currencies.** A pegged rate has near-zero volatility until it does not. Options on pegged currencies price the regime, not the observed variance, and a realised-volatility model calibrated on a peg's history will understate the risk by orders of magnitude.

**Listed FX futures.** A cleared, standardised version of the forward, quoted with the conventionally-based currency as the base regardless of the OTC convention — so a listed contract may be the inverse of the interbank quote for the same pair. Check before hedging one with the other.

**Stablecoins.** On-chain dollar-referenced tokens function as an FX-like market, where the "peg" is a redemption mechanism rather than a central bank commitment, and the deviation from parity reflects redemption friction and counterparty confidence. See [Stablecoins](/building-blocks/stablecoins).

#### Assumptions and Failure Modes

- **Assuming a rate is quoted the way you expect.** The single most common FX error is an inverted quote. Verify direction against a known level before trusting any pipeline.
- **Assuming a 24-hour market is a continuous one.** Liquidity varies by an order of magnitude across the session. An order executed at the Tokyo lunch break and the same order in the London overlap are different trades.
- **Ignoring the roll.** Holding spot beyond its value date incurs the carry, every day, in both directions. A "flat" position that is actually rolling short a high-yielding currency bleeds continuously.
- **Modelling returns without specifying the numeraire.** A 2% gain in EUR/USD is a different number to a euro-based fund and a dollar-based one. Every FX return statement needs a stated reporting currency.
- **Assuming symmetry.** Currency returns have pronounced skew, and the direction of the skew depends on which side of the pair you are on. See [FX Carry and Parity](/markets/fx-carry-parity).
- **Ignoring holidays.** Value date calculations require holiday calendars for both currencies. Getting this wrong produces settlement failures, not mispricing.
